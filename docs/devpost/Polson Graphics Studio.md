# Polson Graphics Studio: Culturally-adaptive, verifiable, agentic graphic and motion design

## What it does
Polson Graphics Studio is an agentic co-creative graphics and motion design platform designed to provide an autonomous, affordable alternative to human-powered on-demand creative service platforms like Design Pickle and Superside, with innovative and accurate graphic and motion design work that adapts to different audiences and languages and cultures all over the world. Polson Graphics Studio provides an accurate, culturally adaptable, alternative to diffusion-model powered image and animation generators like Google Veo or Higgsfield AI Motion Designer for professional-grade image and motion graphics for financial report visualizations, VC pitch decks, film trailers, product advertisments, logos, infographics, posters, and many other types of graphic and motion design projects where accuracy and cultural adaptation is crucual.

Polson attempts to bring the same co-creative environment for agentic software development that modern artificial intelligence enables to the task of visual art and graphic design. Each studio project collects natural language briefs, PDF documents, brand images and other user-supplied assets and follows a formal graphic design workflow, with detailed validation phases desiged to check the accuracy of each fact, number or equation that appears on-screen and delivers raster and vector images and videos in different formats together with creative briefs, brand sheets, mood boards, and many different types of artifacts that are typically the result of the graphic design process.

## Inspiration
### Culture adaptation for graphic design work
In today's  fundamentally globalized and ultra-competitive international markets, brands, studios and founders must now adapt their messaging for many languages, regions, and cultures. Localization of advertising campaigns or investor pitches for different audiences is often an expensive and imprecise undertaking. An agency adapting a campaign for Tokyo and Mumbai or a founder adapting a pitch deck for international investors relies on a strategist's intuition, or on cultural rules of thumb that slide easily into stereotype. The demographics of local communities are diversifying due to migration and other factors. Generative AI can make this worse rather than better: ask a language model what 45-to-54-year-olds in Tokyo watch and it names internationally famous titles. Every localised variant of a graphic or video created by generative AI ends up drawn from the same averaged-out taste. Adapting graphic design for different cultures should be about transformation rather than simply quotation or mimicry.

What is needed for localization and cultural adaptation for graphic design is evidence about the audience that is timely enough to account for shifting demographics and specific enough to design from and inspire a local visual langauage, like films, shows, artists, brands and places that over-index for a place and age band, relative to that place as a whole. For example, an audience that likes fast-paced films or TV could favor a more fast-paced motion design vs. slower-paced videos for other audiences.
![](https://ajb.nyc3.cdn.digitaloceanspaces.com/polson/audience-motion.png)
### The possibility of agentic graphic design
Programmers today are accustomed to collaborating with LLM-based agents on developing software, and the astonishing levels of productivity and creativity that these collaborations enable.  The entire software development industry is being transformed, for better or worse, by the automation of all aspects of software development by agents. But no equivalent creative collaboration and automation has taken root in the field of visual arts or graphic design.

Neural models that generate and manipulate images and other visual artifacts suffer from a core problem in modern artificial intelligence: the innate inability of neural models to handle prolonged, iterative interaction over non-language data that maintans a consistent level of *precision*, *coherency*, and *verifiability*. Language in all its human (and artificial) varieties is discrete, segmentable, addressable. Visual information is continuous, non-segmented, and entangled. When you ask a diffusion-based model like Midjourney or Google's Nano Banana to create or change an image, it follows a completely different process compared to asking an LLM to create or make a change to a program's source code. There are no precise, deterministic tools a diffusion-based model can use to attempt precise operations on an image like "make the hat on the man in the third row red" or "change the figure in the third row to $550,000", in such a way that the objects and properties referred to in the operation retain the permanence humans are accustomed to when thinking and speaking and acting. There are also very limited environmental tools for models to check and validate the text and figures embedded in a visual artifact, the way program code can be checked and validated and tested. This makes agentic graphic design work for things like presentations of financial reports and data very difficult.

An agentic graphic and motion design system that is precise, accurate and reliable enough for medium-horizon tasks like creating videos for VC pitches or analysis of S-1 filings while being able to create design variants that can autonomously adapt to audiences in different cultures or regions requires a fundamentally different approach to agentic systems for image and video generation today. 

Such a system would give ordinary people the power to create professional graphic design productions on their own without needing years of specialized knowledge and training. It would also give professional graphic designers the ability to automate lower-level tasks and projects and allow them to reach higher levels of productivity and creativity and autonomoy.

But such a system has the potential to enable the same revolutionary collaboration and automation for graphic design as exists for software development today.

## How we built it

### Engine

The Polson [drawing engine](https://github.com/allisterb/Polson/blob/master/src/Polson.MCPServer/JsDrawingEngine.cs) is a JavaScript procedural code-as-state drawing engine that uses [Skia](https://skia.org/) for rendering. It has both a 2D vector API compatible with [Snap.svg](https://github.com/adobe-webplatform/Snap.svg), and a 2D raster API with a Canvas2D compatible surface, as well as the conventional Skia 2D API, including Skia [SkSL](https://skia.org/docs/user/sksl/) shaders. The engine does not rely on an embedded browser for executing JavaScript or rendering which allows it to be extremely fast for drawing. Snap.svg and Canvas2D APIs were chosen simply for how much JavaScript code currently exists for drawing using these 2 API shapes that LLMs would be exposed to. Both vector and raster APIs in Polson draw directly to Skia canvases where they can be rendered as bitmaps in different image formats like WebP, or if using the vector API as SVGs that can be consumed by programs like Illustrator or Inkscape.

### Code Mode MCP Server
The Polson drawing engine is exposed to agents as a 'Code Mode' MCP server. Code Mode is a technique for [programmatic tool calling](https://platform.claude.com/cookbook/tool-use-programmatic-tool-calling-ptc) by agents using a code execution environment described by [Cloudfare](https://blog.cloudflare.com/code-mode-mcp/) and [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/code-execution-with-mcp). Instead of requiring the agent to call individual drawing tools one-at-time with a full model round-trip with each result, the agent writes an entire JavaScript program against a typed SDK to complete one stage of the graphic or art production process. 
 

### Workflows
A workflow is the set of inputs, prompts, guardrails, steps, resources, and deliverables an agent uses for one kind of graphic production, e.g the [pitch workflow](https://github.com/allisterb/PolsonFramework/blob/master/src/Polson.CLI/ProjectTemplate/motion/type.pitch.md). Each workflow has a set of stages

It is not a prompt template — it is a generated project directory carrying the instructions, the manuals to consult, the house styles available, the deadline, and the definition of done. Polson currently ships : `vector_infographic`, `infographic`, and `drawing_partner`,

The one worth walking through is **`vector_infographic`**, because it is the one where being wrong is cheapest to hide. A drawing that misses is visibly a miss. An infographic that misstates a number renders perfectly.

![The vector_infographic workflow](https://ajb.nyc3.cdn.digitaloceanspaces.com/polson/vector-infographic-workflow.svg)

The deliverable is an `.svg` that opens in Illustrator with its shapes selectable, its type editable and its geometry scalable — which is why the surface is not a choice: the agent builds on `Snap(width, height)` from the first script and never touches a raster canvas, because a raster canvas cannot become vector afterwards. There is no tracing step. The run is one agent on a 30-minute wall-clock deadline, with a token cap and a circuit breaker behind it, in one of five house styles: `blueprint`, `brutalist`, `editorial`, `specimen`, `swiss`.

## How Polson uses Qloo for culturally adaptive graphic design
Polson uses a .NET client to access the Qloo REST API. The capabilities of the Qloo API are exposed to the agent in the JS SDK via the `Taste` global and functions like `find`, `contrast` etc. that the agent calls before graphic design work for different audiences to establish evidence for things like the pace of videos.
```
Studio request composer
   │  User brief: "Audience A: Tokyo, aged 45 to 54"
   ▼
 Agent
   │  MCP tool call: Taste { op: 'profile', audience: {...} }
   ▼
 MCP server 
   ├─ Taste MCP tool ──► Qloo Insights / Search / Tags / Audiences / Compare
   │                      ├ parameter validator (refuses before sending)
   │                      ├ rate limit, bounded retry, typed failures
   │                      ├ private disk cache, per-session budget
   │                      └ monthly quota read from Qloo's response headers
   ├─ Taste JS global: find / get / pace / contrast / comparePace / images / colourField / oklch / checkReferences
   │     
   │ 
Run record: every query, its purpose, cost, cache status
   │
   ▼
 Studio request pages: Audiences · Contrast · Choices · Verification 
```
### Audience profiles

An audience profile call looks like: 
`Taste({ op: 'profile', audience: { location: 'Tokyo', age: '45_to_54' } })
` 
Agents make multipe profile calls to attempt to determine statistically, for each domain (films, TV, music artists) what this audience loves that its city does not:

1. The audience's list, at a popularity floor of 0.5. Measured with no floor the list is long-tail titles no one would recognise; at 0.9 it is the all-time hits. 0.5 keeps titles that are both recognisable and distinctive.
2. The same query for the place with no age band: the **baseline**.
3. **Distinctive** = in the audience's list and not in the baseline, kept in the audience's own rank order. These are the
   references the design cites. **Shared** = popular there whoever you are.
4. One tag query for the place's aesthetic vocabulary, reported as the *place's*, never the audience's.

That is about seven requests. A second audience in the same city costs four, because the baseline comes from the cache.
If a domain has nothing distinctive, the profile says so, so the agent reports it rather than inventing a difference.

### Audience contrasts

`Taste.contrast(a, b)` compares two audience profiles A and B. It returns:
- titles distinctive to both: evidence for what a series of pieces should share;
- titles distinctive to only A or only B: each piece's own evidence;
- tags leaning each way, with counts;
- A pace comparison for films (see below)
The studio's Contrast tab draws this for each request
![](https://ajb.nyc3.cdn.digitaloceanspaces.com/polson/stuudio_contrast.PNG)

### Pace for motion design
`profile.pace()` counts the audience's distinctive films and shows that Qloo tags fast-paced and slow.
`Taste.comparePace(a, b)` runs **Fisher's exact test** on the two audiences' counts. `comparePaceAll` covers three to six
audiences, Holm-adjusted for the number of pairs.

The rule the workflows enforce: "no measurable difference" is an answer to report, not a reason to invent one. Where pace doesn't differ, the films share a beat grid and differ only where the evidence does (grade, imagery, voice).

### Film audiences
For film posters and trailers 
- **What the film's own fans in this city love** (film posters and trailers). Ask about the audience *with the film as a
  signal*, and subtract the same query without it. A city's whole audience points at its local music; its fans of
  *this* film point somewhere quite different (§8.1).

### Product audiences
- Qloo is used to frtermineWhat buyers of the comparable brands love here, minus what they love anywhere** (product ads). Resolve the brief's
comparable brands, then ask for the brands and artists their buyers like in each city and once globally. What remains
locally is that market's own register. Qloo's brand tags (aesthetic, emotional tone, packaging format) supply the look,
the voice and the packshot. Tags every audience shares are treated as the category's look, not as a difference. Qloo's place results (with a location filter) supply each city's setting and its mood tags.

### Visual research from Qloo data
`Taste.images(profile, { type: 'film' })` fetches the posters of an audience's distinctive films, or the logos of its
buyers' brands. `Taste.colourField(pictures)` measures each one in OKLCH and maps where their hues crowd on the colour
wheel and where it is empty. This is the method in Ellen Lupton's book called "Visual Research": collect a field, measure it, and decide whether the new piece should stand in it or apart from it. At the end, `Taste.oklch` places
the finished piece's colours on the same wheel. A `Stage.check` then confirms the piece stands where the direction said
it would.

### 7.1 Film poster (the lead)

Nine stages after Laserow & Delgado's *Making Posters*. The Qloo and research layers are ours.

1. **Audience.** A profile per audience, its place checked. The director is asked whether any distinctive reference would
   be wrong to borrow from.
2. **The film's own fans** in each city (§6.4), and `demographics` on the film to confirm the age skew.
3. **One `compare`** per audience: its references against the rest of the set's.
4. **Mind map and research board.** Each audience gets a branch of its distinctive titles and tags, each cited with its
   handle. The map is checked: a node cited to an answer that didn't return it is fixed or dropped. Then the colour field
   of the audience's own films (§6.5).
5. **Concepts.** Three per audience, each starting from a different item in that audience's profile, named.
   Later audiences' concepts are drawn from their own profile **before** looking back at the first.
6. **Direction.** One section per audience; each section after the first opens with what its poster does differently,
   and which of its own profile's items caused each difference.
7. **The series** holds the system (grid, type, title) and never the image, composition or palette. Those are what each
   audience's evidence decides.
8. **The set test.** All finals side by side. If a stranger couldn't tell them apart, the findings say so *"rather than
   forcing a difference the evidence does not support"*.
9. **Before delivery:** the colour check against the field, and `Taste.checkReferences()`.

### 7.2 Product ad (poster or film)

**"Dynamic creative"**: one template, with each variant being what its audience's evidence decides.

- **Evidence.** The buyers-here-minus-buyers-anywhere method (§6.4). The opening beat of the film is
  *"the one image from this audience's evidence — the setting, the hour, the object"*.
- **Pace** comes from the audience's own profile, **not** from the buyers' films. We measured that: buyers' pace changed
  with how many titles were read (33% fast at 25, 59% at 50), and no pair of cities differed.
- **Comparables are signals, never imagery.** No logo, trade dress or signature colour is borrowed.
- **The claim** comes only from the brief, and is read back off the finished film word for word.
- **Age- or place-restricted products** go to the director: *"Qloo measures taste; it knows nothing about the law."*

### 7.3 Film trailer, startup pitch, report explainer

Motion needs evidence a poster doesn't, because *"a poster's evidence decides what the film looks like; nothing in it
decides how the film moves"*.

- **Pace** comes from `pace()` and the pace comparison (§6.3). With several audiences, every profile is fetched and
  compared before any beat is planned.
- **Grade** comes from the distinctive titles' looks.
- **Tempo in beats per minute** is explicitly *not* a Qloo fact. Without a source it is chosen and marked *assumed*.
- **The finals.** One build function makes every audience's film (`final_a.mp4`, `final_b.mp4`, …). Where a pair
  differs in pace, a check confirms the films really do: number of arrival beats, median hold, longest still stretch,
  end-card hold. A "Between the audiences" section says what is shared and why, and what differs with which evidence.
- **Pitch and report films.** The case and the facts are shared; *"only its telling is the audience's"*. Number
  formats follow the locale's own rules (Unicode CLDR), labelled *Rule*, not Qloo.

## 8. What Qloo changed in real runs

From the studio's own run records (`direction.md` and the agent's `findings.md`). These are the cases a judge can open
on the demo.

### 8.1 *Be Cool* posters, Tokyo 45–54 and Mumbai 45–54 (poster5)

- **Pace became composition.** Of each audience's distinctive films and shows, Qloo tags Tokyo's 14 fast and 12 slow
  (54%), and Mumbai's 23 fast and 5 slow (82%). Fisher's exact test: *p* = 0.040. The Mumbai poster got a radial burst;
  the Tokyo poster a left-weighted vertical. The agent: *"I would not have inverted the scale of the subject between two
  posters of one set on instinct; a p-value made it defensible."*
- **The film's fans are not the city.** Without the film as a signal, each city's artists are its own local music. With
  it, Tokyo's fans point to golden-age hip hop and funk, Mumbai's to Motown, disco and adult pop. *"Had I designed from
  the profiles alone I would have made a poster about Japanese city pop…"*
- **A measurement overruled the designer.** The agent's instinct for Tokyo was amber or sodium orange. The colour field
  of that audience's sixteen distinctive film posters had exactly two empty hue sectors, teal and green, so the poster is
  teal: *"because of a measurement that contradicted me."* For Mumbai, red-orange held 9 of 16 posters and none of the 16
  was light, so that poster sits in the crowded hue but departs in value, on a cream ground.
- **The age band was checked.** `demographics` on the film confirmed its skew to 45–54.

### 8.2 *Be Cool* trailers, the same two audiences (trailer5)

The same pace test, on film:

| | Tokyo | Mumbai |
|---|---|---|
| Arrival beats | 6 | 8 |
| Median hold | 2.75 s | 1.75 s |
| Title lands at | 10.9 s | 5.0 s |

A `Stage.check` confirmed the difference across the whole film. The grades come from each audience's films' style tags:
gentle for Tokyo, where the lean was mixed; warmer, crisper and more saturated for Mumbai, whose "Glitzy" tag also
motivated a glint beat.
